Pricing only feels awkward when the operator is still trying to defend it. The better posture is simpler. Explain the options clearly, tell the truth about what each one is for, and recommend the tier that fits the work.

That is the job of this module.

The current numbers and exact plan terms live in the source-of-truth docs. Those can change. What should not change is how you talk someone through the choices in plain language.

---

## Start with fit, not price

The first question is not "what can they afford?" The first question is "what kind of build is this?"

Keiter scopes by the work the site needs to do.

That means the tier follows from things like:

- page count and content depth
- number of distinct visitor paths
- whether the business needs a straightforward brochure site or something more custom
- whether the site needs special functionality
- whether the project is mostly clarity and structure, or whether it asks for deeper custom build work

If you lead with fit, the pricing conversation stays grounded.

---

## The three build tiers

The curriculum has already named the three core build tiers: Starter, Standard, and Custom.

### Starter

Starter is for simpler sites with a tight scope. Fewer pages. A clear primary action. No major custom functionality. The business still gets a professional build, just without the extra structure a more complex site needs.

Starter is the right fit when the project can stay disciplined. It is the wrong fit when intake already shows multiple visitor paths, a larger content story, or likely growth pressure.

### Standard

Standard is where many Keiter builds belong. It is for a fuller marketing and lead-capture site with real structure, several pages, and enough thinking behind it that a one-page build would leave useful material on the floor.

The Outpost Vending example in Module 09 is a good model. Starter was too small because the business had distinct visitor paths and a real story to tell. Custom was more than the project needed. Standard was the right container.

### Custom

Custom is for projects where the work no longer fits cleanly inside the more defined packages. That might be deeper functionality, more complex information architecture, or technical requirements beyond the usual marketing-site lane.

Custom is not the prestige tier. It is the right-fit tier when the work genuinely asks for it.

<Callout variant="key-rule">
  Do not upsell someone into a bigger tier to make the invoice larger. Recommend the tier that fits the site the business actually needs.
</Callout>

## How to explain the tiers in plain language

A small business owner should be able to hear the explanation once and understand where they fall.

Plain-language version:

- Starter is the lean, focused option.
- Standard is the fuller business site most growing businesses need.
- Custom is for projects with special requirements that go beyond the normal package shapes.

That is usually enough to start. After that, explain why you are recommending the tier you are recommending in terms of the business itself, not in terms of package marketing.

Example:

> Based on what you described, you have more than one type of visitor coming to this site, and they need different information. That pushes this out of Starter. You do not need a custom application, so Standard is the right fit.

That kind of explanation builds trust because it is about the work, not a sales script.

## Care Plans are ongoing support, not an add-on afterthought

Care Plans exist because launch is not the end of the relationship. Sites need hosting, maintenance, small updates, monitoring, and a clear home for ongoing questions.

The exact active Care Plan lineup lives in the source docs, and that lineup can evolve over time. The job of the operator is to explain the function of the plan the client is choosing.

Good plain-language explanation:

> Your site needs a home, a maintenance rhythm, and a clear way to handle normal changes without starting a new project every time. The Care Plan is that ongoing container.

When you explain Care Plans, cover:

- what ongoing support is included
- what kind of updates fit inside the plan
- what would count as a new scoped project instead
- how communication works once the site is live

That keeps expectations clean from the start.

## The no-upfront plan

The no-upfront plan changes payment timing, not the standard of the work.

That matters because some prospects hear "no upfront" and assume it means stripped-down service or loose expectations. It does not. The build still needs the same clarity, the same documents, and the same standards.

What changes is how the project is financed.

You should explain it plainly:

- this is a payment structure, not a lower-quality build
- not every project is a fit for it
- the details and terms come from the current source-of-truth docs, not from memory

If you are ever fuzzy on the active terms, stop and verify before quoting them. Pricing details are not something to freestyle.

---

## The grandfather clause

The spec names the grandfather clause because it matters in real conversations. Plain-language version: existing clients should not be surprised by changing plan terms that were never discussed with them.

When someone is grandfathered, you explain that their current arrangement stays tied to the terms they came in under, unless Keiter has a direct conversation with them about changing it.

That protects trust. It also keeps the business from creating confusion by talking about current plan structure as though it automatically rewrites older agreements.

## How to scope the recommendation

When you recommend a tier, show your work.

Questions that help:

- How many distinct pages or content sections does this business really need?
- Is there one clear visitor path, or several?
- Does the business need special functionality, or just clear structure?
- Is the project likely to outgrow the smallest tier quickly?
- Is there anything about the voice, brand, or content load that demands a fuller build shape?

Then say the recommendation simply.

<Callout variant="example" title="Simple tier recommendation">
  You do not need Custom. What you described is a real multi-page business site with a few distinct visitor paths, which puts you squarely in Standard. Starter would make us leave too much useful information out.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes in pricing conversations

### Talking in package language instead of business language

Clients do not care that a tier is called Standard. They care what it means for their business.

### Recommending too small a build to keep the number comfortable

That feels easier in the moment and creates pain later.

### Quoting terms from memory

Use the source docs for current pricing and payment details. Always.

### Talking about Care Plans like an upsell

They are part of responsible site ownership. Explain them that way.

---

## What you want the client to feel

By the end of the conversation, the client should feel three things:

- they understand the options
- they understand why you recommended the one you did
- they do not feel talked around

That is the quality bar here. Clear, direct, no posturing.
