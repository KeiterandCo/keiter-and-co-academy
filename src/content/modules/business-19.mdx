Launch is not the moment you disappear. It is the moment the client should feel steady.

The site is live, yes. But the handoff matters just as much as the deploy. A client should leave launch knowing what they own, where things live, what happens next, and how to ask for help without feeling silly.

That is what the Welcome to the Family guide is for.

---

## Launch starts before the deploy

If launch day feels frantic, something upstream is still loose.

Before you point traffic at the live site, confirm:

- the final review is complete
- the client approved the last round of changes
- forms, links, and mobile navigation work on the real URL
- domain and DNS are stable
- analytics or other environment variables are in place if the build uses them
- the Welcome guide is drafted and ready to hand over

The guide should not be an afterthought you assemble after the client already has questions.

<Callout variant="key-rule">
  Do not treat launch as a technical finish line only. It is a client experience moment.
</Callout>

---

## In person is the preferred handoff when it works

The kickoff language is clear here. Keiter prefers a real handoff where possible.

That does not mean every launch must happen across a table. Schedules, distance, and family life are real. But if you can sit down with the client, walk through the site together, and hand over the guide as a real artifact, do it.

Why that matters:

- the client can ask questions in the moment
- you can watch where they hesitate
- ownership feels real, not abstract
- the relationship moves from project into ongoing support cleanly

If the handoff is remote, keep the same spirit. Screen share. Slow down. Leave space for questions.

---

## What the Welcome guide is doing

The guide says, in effect, "This is your site. Here is how it works. Here is what Keiter is still handling. Here is how to reach us when something comes up."

It is not a developer document. It is not a dump of credentials with no context. It is a plain-language ownership guide.

That means the writing has to stay human. If the guide reads like software documentation, the client will avoid it until something is wrong.

---

## Walk through the guide section by section

The exact template can evolve, but the operator should be able to explain each section and why it exists.

### 1. Quick orientation

Start with a warm opening that names what just happened. The site is live. The project is complete. The guide is the client's reference point.

This section lowers the temperature. It tells the client they do not need to remember everything from the launch conversation.

### 2. Site overview

Give the client a simple map of what was built.

Name the key pages, the main goal of the site, and any important conversion path like a contact form, booking link, or menu download.

### 3. Where things live

This section covers the accounts and systems attached to the site.

That can include:

- domain registrar
- hosting platform
- DNS management
- form destination
- analytics property, if present
- any third-party tools tied to the site

The important thing is clarity about ownership and access.

<Callout variant="source-of-truth" title="Source-of-truth habit">
  The guide should point back to the live system of record when needed. If the domain is at Namecheap, name Namecheap. If hosting is at Netlify, name Netlify. Do not turn the guide into a vague summary that hides where the real controls are.
</Callout>

### 4. Editing basics

If the client is expected to change anything themselves, explain the safe path in plain language.

Examples:

- how to update a phone number or hours if the site supports that
- what kind of changes should come back to Keiter instead
- what not to touch without checking first

This section exists to prevent accidental breakage and reduce client anxiety.

### 5. Care Plan and support path

The guide should clearly name whether the client is on a Care Plan, what that includes at a high level, and how requests should be sent.

If there is no ongoing plan, say that plainly too. Silence here creates confusion.

### 6. Launch-day checklist and next steps

Close by naming the first things that happen after launch.

That can include:

- checking the live site on a phone
- sharing the new URL in the right places
- watching for early form submissions
- sending over any final assets or records still in motion

The point is to show the client that launch is orderly.

---

## The guide is an ownership artifact

One reason Keiter includes this step so explicitly is that many small business owners have had digital work done for them without ever feeling like they actually owned the result.

They got a website, but not the map.

They got credentials, but not the explanation.

They got told to reach out anytime, but not a clear operating path.

The Welcome guide fixes that. It makes the handoff legible.

---

## A clean launch rhythm

Here is the practical sequence:

1. Final pre-launch review
2. Production deploy
3. Domain and smoke test confirmation
4. Client walkthrough
5. Welcome guide handoff
6. Clear note on what ongoing support looks like

If the order gets muddled, the client feels it.

---

## What to avoid at launch

### Too much jargon

If a client has to decode the guide, it is not doing its job.

### Credentials with no explanation

A list of logins is not a handoff.

### Promising ongoing support vaguely

If support exists, say what the path is. If it does not, say that clearly.

### Treating the guide as optional

The guide is part of the build. It is not bonus polish.

---

## Why this belongs in the business curriculum

Launch is where trust either consolidates or slips.

If the client leaves launch thinking, "I know what I own, I know what Keiter is still handling, and I know where to look when I forget something," then the handoff worked.

That feeling is part of the service.
