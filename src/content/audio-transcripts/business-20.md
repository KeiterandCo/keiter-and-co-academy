Care Plans are where the long-term relationship becomes visible.

This is not a line item you tack on after launch because recurring revenue sounds nice. It is the operating structure for ongoing support, hosting, updates, monitoring, and real communication after the site is live.

If a launch says, your site is live, the Care Plan says, you are not on your own now.

What a Care Plan is for

Module 16 covers how to explain Care Plans. This module covers how to run one.

In practice, a Care Plan usually means:

- hosting stays stable
- the site gets watched, not ignored
- there is a clear path for small tasks and questions
- the client hears from Keiter regularly enough to know the relationship is active
- changes in scope get handled cleanly instead of leaking into unlimited support

The important shift is this: after launch, you are no longer in build mode. You are in stewardship mode.

The monthly rhythm should feel calm

The best ongoing support does not feel dramatic. It feels steady.

At a minimum, the operator should have a consistent monthly rhythm for:

- analytics or visibility review
- one small maintenance or content task when the plan includes it
- checking for any problems the client has not surfaced yet
- communicating in plain language about what happened that month

The client should not have to wonder whether anyone is paying attention.

Hosting and maintenance are part of the relationship

If Keiter is hosting the site, that carries operational responsibility.

That includes:

- keeping the hosting setup healthy
- watching for broken contact paths or major issues
- knowing where the domain and DNS live
- making sure recurring tools tied to the site still work as expected

The hosting model is not just billing. It is care.

Monthly communication should sound like a person wrote it

A good monthly note is short, clear, and useful.

It should usually answer four questions:

1. What happened on the site this month?
2. What did Keiter do?
3. Is anything worth watching next month?
4. Is there a clear next step, if one exists?

The client does not need a dashboard dump. They need a translated summary.

  If the monthly note sounds like software talking about performance metrics, rewrite it until it sounds like Olivia explaining it across a table.

Small tasks need a boundary

Care Plans work because they are supportive and bounded at the same time.

Examples of a good monthly small task:

- updating seasonal hours
- swapping one photo
- editing a short paragraph
- posting a prepared event update

Examples of work that should trigger a scope conversation:

- adding a new page that changes site structure
- rewriting core positioning across the whole site
- integrating a new third-party system
- expanding the site into a materially different build

Do not let long-term support turn into silent re-scoping.

Review monitoring and ongoing watchfulness

Part of ongoing care is noticing what the client does not always notice first.

That can include:

- checking whether lead flow looks normal
- watching for obvious broken links or form issues
- keeping an eye on search visibility trends at a plain-language level
- noticing when public business details changed and the site may need an update

This is not obsessive monitoring. It is attentive stewardship.

Scope-change conversations should be clean and early

The longer you wait to name a scope change, the harder the conversation gets.

If a client starts asking for work outside the normal plan rhythm, say so plainly:

"That makes sense as a next step. It sits outside the small ongoing updates we have been handling, so let me scope that properly for you."

That is direct, respectful, and easier on everyone than quietly absorbing it until resentment builds.

Offboarding should feel orderly, not punitive

Sometimes a Care Plan ends. That can happen because the client is moving to a different setup, taking updates in-house, pausing the business, or simply changing direction.

The Keiter standard is clean offboarding.

That means:

- confirm the end date clearly
- confirm who will own hosting, domain, DNS, and any related accounts after the transition
- deliver any credentials or records that belong with the client
- note any open items that will not be handled after the end date
- leave the relationship in a state that reflects well on Keiter

  Offboarding is where ownership details matter most. Name the real systems, the real dates, and the real handoff path. Vagueness here creates future support problems.

A simple monthly operator checklist

1. Review site health and the main lead path
2. Review plain-language performance signals
3. Complete the included small task, if there is one
4. Send the monthly snapshot
5. Flag anything that is drifting into a scope change
6. Record anything the next month will need

That rhythm keeps the work legible.

Why this matters

Client trust is not built only during intake and build. It is either reinforced or weakened in the quiet months after launch.

If support is clear, warm, and bounded, the relationship stays healthy.

If support is vague, reactive, or resentful, the relationship frays.

Care Plans in practice are about keeping that from happening.
