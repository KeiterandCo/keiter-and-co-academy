The proposal should be one page or less

The kickoff is explicit here. The Keiter format is one page or less, three sections, warm and clear.

That constraint is useful. It forces the proposal to name the decision cleanly instead of burying it in paragraphs.

The proposal is not the contract. It is the clear business summary that helps the client understand what they are saying yes to.

A simple three-part proposal structure

1. What the business needs
2. What Keiter is proposing to build
3. Investment, timing, and the next step

That is enough for most small business projects.

Section one, what the business needs

Start by reflecting the project back in plain language. This is where the client should feel seen.

Name the real problem:

- what the site needs to do
- who it needs to serve
- what feels unclear or outdated today
- what a better outcome looks like

This section should sound like it came from the intake conversation, because it did.

Section two, what Keiter is proposing

Now name the recommendation.

This is where you explain the tier, the overall site shape, and any major included pieces. Keep it concrete. If the proposal says "comprehensive digital presence" and nothing more, it is too vague.

Better:

> We are recommending a Standard build with a focused home page, an About page that tells the real story of the business, a contact path that makes inquiry easy, and the supporting pages needed to answer common customer questions clearly.

That gives the client a mental picture.

Section three, investment, timing, next step

End with the practical pieces:

- the investment
- the payment structure
- expected timing
- what the client does next if they want to move forward

This section should remove friction, not add mystery.

If there is a Care Plan choice attached, name it clearly. If the no-upfront plan is the agreed structure, say so clearly. If timing depends on a client deliverable, say that too.

Payment edge cases

Small businesses do not live in perfect conditions. You need a calm way to handle the messy parts.

Late payments

Stay direct. Reference the agreement. Keep the tone human, not passive-aggressive. The goal is clarity, not punishment.

Refund questions

Do not improvise. This is where the standing principle "we are not lawyers" matters. Follow the actual agreement and stop to verify if anything is unclear.

Scope changes

Treat them as scope changes, not small favors hidden inside the original agreement. If the work changed, name that clearly and discuss the adjustment directly.

Contract confusion

If a client is unsure what they are agreeing to, the answer is not more pressure. The answer is a clearer explanation.

What a clean handoff into build looks like

Before the build begins, the operator should be able to say yes to all of these:

- the project scope is clear
- the client accepted the agreement through the intended path
- the payment step is documented
- the recommended tier and timeline are recorded
- the next build documents can start from a stable footing

That last point matters. Intake leads to proposal. Proposal leads to agreement and payment. Then the three-document system starts from clarity rather than guesswork.
